Connected and Autonomous Vehicles

Set-Based Digital Engineering for Adaptive and Highly Automated Off-Road Military Ground Vehicles:

In recent years, the U.S. Army has prioritized digital engineering (reduced reliance on physical prototyping) and reduced development timelines, all while the future battlefield has grown more uncertain. One mechanism for maintaining high performance in the presence of uncertainty, arising both from imperfect physical models at the time of design freezes and uncertain battlespace conditions, is through the incorporation of real-time adaptation into ground vehicle designs. Adaptation can come in many forms, from discrete reconfigurability (e.g., a modular payload set or finite selection of tires), to continuous reconfigurability (e.g., a continously variable suspension damping coefficient or continuously variable ride height), to adaptive control (i.e., software parameters that are adjusted in real time). An example of various modes of adaptation is shown below, for a CAD model based off of the U.S. Army Joint Light Tactical Vehicle (JLTV).

Mathematically speaking, incorporating adaptability into a design means that, at the time of deployment, the design will not comprise a single point but will rather comprise a set of points (which may be continuous or disjoint, and maybe continuously modifiable or only modifiable at discrete points in time). In fact, a field of multidisciplinary design optimization (MDO) known as set-based design, has examined design processes wherein the design is reduced to a set at early points in the cycle but ultimately reduced to a point at the time of deployment. With adaptability, however, the design remains a set even at the time of deployment, leading to new mathematical challenges associated with the design optimization.

Game-Theoretic Learning Strategies for Optimizing Human-Vehicle Interaction in Shared Control Settings:

Shared control involves collaboration between autonomy and a human driver, where the inputs from both entities are blended through what is known as an arbitration strategy. While most arbitration strategies in shared control are fixed, remaining consistent regardless of who is driving and where they happen to be driving, the reality is that differences in individual driver behavior and route segments motivate adaptation strategies for tailoring the arbitration to the driver and location. Through work sponsored by the U.S. Army Automotive Research Center (ARC), our team is exploring iterative learning strategies for tailoring arbitration strategies on repeated routes. In particular, these routes are divided into segments, where the relationships of the segments to each other are described through a correlation matrix. Gradient-based and Bayesian optimization-based algorithms are being explored for carrying out the learning step. Meanwhile, additional research is focusing on utilizing game theory — particularly within the realm of cognitive hierarchy — to better characterize the human-vehicle interaction and enable a combination of vehicle-level learning and driver training within the iterative learning process.

Collaborative and Predictive Routing, Speed, and Powertrain Control for Fuel Economy Optimization:

Together with collaborators at Penn State, MIT, and Volvo Group North America, our team at NS State developed predictive control algorithms that use real-time data from the target vehicle and surrounding vehicles to optimize the route (subject to start and end points), vehicle speed (subject to arrival time constraint windows), and powertrain control strategies. The goal of this optimization was to achieve 20% total fuel economy improvement through the combined optimization at four levels – route control, vehicle control, transmission control, and engine/accessory control. The investigated control strategies are applicable to model year 2016 internal combustion vehicles; through its collaboration with Volvo Group North America, the research team have performed simulation and experimental validation studies of its proposed solutions on a VNL 300 heavy diesel truck.
